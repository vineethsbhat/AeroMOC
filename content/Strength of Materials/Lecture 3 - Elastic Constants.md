### Engineering Stress and Strain

The development of elastic constants like E (Young’s Modulus), K (Bulk Modulus), and G (Shear Modulus) was driven by the **"Uniconstant Controversy."**

### Young’s Modulus (E)

Though named after **Thomas Young** (1807), the concept was actually played with by **Leonhard Euler** decades earlier. Young's contribution was formalizing E as a property of the material itself, independent of its shape. It tells you how "stiff" a material is when you pull it

### The Drama of K and G

In the early 1820s, **Claude-Louis Navier** and Cauchy developed a "molecular" theory of elasticity. They assumed that materials were made of atoms that only pulled on each other directly. Their math suggested that every isotropic material (like steel or glass) could be described by just **one** constant. This implied that a material's **Poisson’s Ratio** (ν) _must_ always be exactly 0.25.

**George Green** and **Gabriel Lamé** proved that you need at least **two** independent constants to describe an isotropic material. This gave way to:

- **Shear Modulus (G or μ):** Formalized by Lamé to describe resistance to changing shape (twisting/shearing).

- **Bulk Modulus (K):** Developed to describe resistance to changing volume (uniform compression).

These constants are all mathematically linked. For example, if you know E and G, you can calculate K using the formula:

$$K = \frac{EG}{3(3G-E)}$$
The Industrial Revolution (think: steam engines and iron bridges) made these constants vital. Engineers needed to know exactly how much a bridge would sag or a boiler would expand before they actually built them.
